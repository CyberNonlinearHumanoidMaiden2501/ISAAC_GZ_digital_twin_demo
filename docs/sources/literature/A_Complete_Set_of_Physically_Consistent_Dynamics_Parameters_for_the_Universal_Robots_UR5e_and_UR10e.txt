ABSTRACT Complete, physically consistent dynamics parameters are required for forward dynamics
simulation and model-based control methods that explicitly use a separated generalized mass matrix.
Complete dynamics parameters are not provided by the manufacturer. The available CAD models contain
neither closed surfaces (and thus no volumetric information) nor density information from which the required
mass properties could be determined reliably. This paper reports complete reference parameter sets for
the Universal Robots UR5e and UR10e using an established physically consistent identification approach.
To obtain representative results, two UR5e and four UR10e robots are identified, and both robot-specific
and averaged parameter sets are reported. The identified inertia parameters agree well among the robots,
whereas the friction parameters vary. For the evaluated UR10e trajectory, the torque differences between
the robot-specific and averaged models remain within approximately 5 % of the maximum joint torques,
supporting the use of the averaged sets as nominal reference parameters. A forward dynamics simulation
further demonstrates the use of the complete parameter sets in model-based control. For the evaluated
slow pick-and-place motion, the Coriolis and centrifugal torque contributions are sufficiently small to be
neglected. The computational benefit is assessed separately: omitting these terms reduces the mean model
evaluation time from 8.9 µs to 3.3 µs per sample. Although the averaged parameter sets provide useful
nominal models, robot-specific friction identification may still be required depending on the specific task.
Practical details relevant to reproducing the identification are also discussed. This paper should serve the
robotics community as a reliable and representative reference for physically consistent dynamics parameters
of the UR5e and UR10e robots.

(EOM) with a positive definite generalized mass matrix M,
and thus the full set of inertia parameters. The EOM are
the basis for forward dynamics simulations and stability

M are crucial. All established robot simulation frameworks,
such as Pinocchio [6], Gazebo [7], and ROS [8], rely on

of physical human–robot interaction (pHRI). The so-called
reflected mass [9], [10], [11], i.e., the robot inertia apparent
at the end-effector (EE), requires the mass matrix. It is known
that the mass matrix M deduced from base-parameters is
not guaranteed to be positive definite [12], [13], [14], and
negative values for the rotor inertia may also be obtained [15],
violating positive definiteness. Such physical inconsistencies
lead to unstable forward dynamics simulation [16], and
unstable model-based control [14]. The important point here
is that the EOM, and a positive definite mass matrix, can
only be constructed from the full set of physically consistent
dynamics parameters p. The consistency of inertia parameters
implies a positive mass m and a positive-definite inertia tensor
2 fulfilling the triangle inequalities for all bodies. Consistent
friction coefficients need to be positive. With the full set
of dynamics parameters, joint reaction forces can also be
computed and considered in motion planning.
Several methods for identification of the full set of
parameters were proposed. Physical consistency is enforced
by means of linear inequality constraints on rigid body
parameters [17], [18], [19], [20], including a penalty term
in the objective function [21], or manifold theory [22].
In [23] a non-linear parameter transformation is proposed
to encode the consistency conditions. The advantage of this
method is that this leads to an unconstrained optimization
problem. Identified dynamics parameters have been reported
for the Franka Emika Panda [21], the Kuka iiwa robot [24],
the Kinova Jaco2 [25], and the Barrett WAM arm [17]. For
the UR5e and UR10e robots, only identified base-parameters
have been reported [15], [26], [27], [28]. Although geometric
dimensions can be extracted from the manufacturer-provided
CAD models, the link geometries do not consist of closed
surfaces. Consequently, the volumes, centers of mass, and
inertia tensors cannot be determined reliably from these
models. Moreover, complete dynamics parameters are not
provided by the manufacturer and, to the best of the authors’
knowledge, have not been reported elsewhere for the UR5e
and UR10e.
The main contribution of this paper is a validated reference
dataset comprising complete, physically consistent dynamics
parameters for the UR5e and UR10e. The identification
builds on an established physically consistent parameterization and incorporates robot-specific model assumptions to
reduce the number of optimization variables. Two UR5e and
four UR10e robots are identified, and both robot-specific
and averaged parameter sets are reported. The averaged sets

II. INVERSE DYNAMICS MODEL

the control force for a given trajectory. Such a model is
used to compute the feed-forward term in a model-based
control scheme. A central result is that this model is linear
in the dynamics parameters, which is due to the fact that
the kinetic energy is linear in the inertia parameters and the
friction models used are linear in the velocity. The inverse

C. INVERSE DYNAMICS MODEL IN TERMS OF
BASE-PARAMETERS

Since not all parameters can be identified independently, the
regressor matrix Y(q, q̇, q̈) is rank deficient [1]. Numerical
methods, like QR-factorization and SVD, are used to obtain
an independent set of so-called base-parameters, denoted pB .
The inverse dynamics model is then transformed to τ =
YB pB , where YB is a regular matrix obtained from Y, and
pB is a vector of linearly independent base-parameters. For
this decomposition, the regressor matrix Y is evaluated at
random realizations of the joint angles q, velocities q̇, and
accelerations q̈ within their respective range, and stacked at
least (10N + 3N )/N times in a matrix Ȳ. The corresponding
stacked joint torque vector is τ̄ . QR factorization of this
stacked regressor matrix yields Ȳ = QR, and the matrix
ȲB is obtained by removing the columns of Q corresponding
to numerical zeros on the main diagonal of R. It should be
noted that neither the QR factorization nor the SVD is unique;
hence, the derived set of base-parameters is not unique.

V. PRACTICAL ASPECTS FOR IMPLEMENTATION

Successful application of the proposed identification procedure requires several practical considerations. These include
trajectory control and data recording, data filtering, fading
in and out of the excitation trajectory, determining the
regularization values, and preparing the experiments to
ensure repeatability.
A. TRAJECTORY CONTROL AND RECORDING

The robots used for identification differ in their installation
setup, rate of use, and control and communication systems.
Two UR5e robots, denoted UR5e-1 and UR5e-2, and four
UR10e robots, referred to as UR10e-1 to UR10e-4, were
used.
Both UR5e robots were operated at 500 Hz using the
libraries from SDU Robotics [42] on a PC running Ubuntu
with a real-time kernel. Using this library, the control
commands were continuously sent to and measurements were
obtained from the UR controller. This communication link
has well-known issues, e.g. real-time operation is violated
when the UR’s internal controller cannot handle the control
requests. This was observed in the measurements. Occasionally, samples were lost, and the timestamps of consecutive
samples were inconsistent. Reducing the sampling frequency
to fS = 125 Hz reduced these errors, but did not eliminate
them completely. Furthermore, physically impossible outliers
were observed in the motor torque measurements. These
outliers had no noticeable consequences for the identification
results.
The UR10e-2 was controlled using a custom ROS
framework, with a control node implemented in C++, and
138711

D. Gnad et al.: Complete Set of Physically Consistent Dynamics Parameters

running on Ubuntu with a real-time kernel. The desired joint
velocities were sent to the robot at a frequency of 500 Hz by
publishing the commands on the joint_group_vel ROS topic.
The measurements of the joint angles, velocities, and motor
currents were exported by subscribing to the joint_states ROS
topic.
For the UR10e-1, UR10e-3, and UR10e-4 robots, communication between the UR’s internal controller and an external
PC was avoided. The joint angles of the desired trajectory
were sampled and written to a UR-script using the servoj
command. A look-ahead horizon of 30 ms and a gain of
1500 were used as settings for this control command. The
generated text file was loaded as a program directly using
the UR’s teach pendant (hand-held control device). The
measurements were accessed using the RTDE library and a
buffer to avoid data loss.
It is concluded that the real-time communication with
the UR controller is the most volatile approach. The RTDE
library (which must be used in all control schemes) did
not provide a reliable real-time-capable connection for data
recording. The observed time differences of consecutive
samples and unrealistic outliers of the motor torque measurement require manual post-processing (smoothing and lowpass filtering). The RTDE library can still be used for data
recording, but only in combination with a buffer. No missing
time samples or outliers were observed for this setup. The
described ROS communication achieved a sufficiently good
connection during trajectory tracking and data recording.
No outliers of the motor torque measurement were observed.
In conclusion, creating a UR program to be executed by
the robot is deemed to be the most reliable approach for
the purpose of identifying dynamics parameters. In that
case, the only required hardware for the data recording task
is a standard office PC. It should be remarked that the
identification procedure does not rely on accurate tracking
of the identification trajectories. The important condition is
merely that the actual executed motion (joint trajectory q(t)
and the corresponding torques τ (t)) can be measured.
B. DATA FILTERING

The regressor matrix Y(q, q̇, q̈) depends on the joint angles,
velocities, and accelerations. The RTDE library provides
access to the internal time vector and measurements of
the joint angles, velocities, and motor currents, among
other quantities, for all setups. The joint acceleration has
to be derived by numerical differentiation. The MATLAB
command filtfilt, applied with a realizable first-order differentiation filter provides a convenient way to compensate
for the effect of group delay. To cope with the previously
described outliers and the effects of numerical differentiation,
a smoothing filter (moving average filter) was applied to the
derived joint accelerations and motor current measurements.
The motor torque constants used for the UR5e and UR10e
and the known gear ratios iG = 101 (the same gear ratio for
all joint sizes) are used to obtain the actuation torques τ .

C. FADING IN/OUT OF THE EXCITATION TRAJECTORY

The optimized excitation trajectory is described by a
truncated Fourier series. Accordingly, the joint velocities
and accelerations are non-zero at the start and end of the
trajectory. A smooth transition from standstill to the trajectory
is required to avoid the excitation of unwanted vibrations.
Some authors [27], [43] impose additional constraints on
the Fourier coefficients for the optimization to obtain zero
velocity at the beginning and end of the excitation trajectory.
However, this may still lead to high accelerations at the start
and end of the trajectory, and to unwanted vibrations. This
can be avoided by smoothing the excitation trajectory at the
start and end.
An efficient strategy is to (re)parameterize the excitation
trajectory [44] using a path parameter σ (t) which represents
a transformed time. This yields the joint trajectory
q = q(σ ), q̇ = q′ (σ )σ̇ , q̈ = q′′ (σ )σ̇ 2 + q′ (σ )σ̈ ,
where ()′ denotes the derivative w.r.t. the path parameter
σ and ()˙ the derivative w.r.t. time t. The derivatives
w.r.t. the path parameter are directly available from (12).
At start and end of the trajectory, σ̇ (t) < 1 is required,
while during the trajectory, σ̇ (t) = 1 holds. Various
time-scaling methods fulfilling these requirements have been
reported [45]. A trajectory with sin2 acceleration profile for
the path parameter with σ̇max = 1 s−1 , σ̈max = 4 s−2 was
used.

VII. CONCLUSION

This paper presents complete, physically consistent inertial
parameter sets for the UR5e and UR10e robots. These
parameter sets address an important practical limitation,
as the inertial properties cannot be reliably obtained from
the publicly available manufacturer CAD models, which lack
material density and volumetric information. The parameters
were identified using a physically consistent inertial parameterization together with structural assumptions that exploit
the mechanical design of the robots while preserving physical
feasibility. Unlike base-parameter sets, which are primarily
intended for inverse dynamics, the proposed parameters
provide complete body-specific inertial properties that are
directly compatible with URDF-based rigid-body models.
The resulting generalized mass matrices are guaranteed to
be symmetric and positive definite, making the identified
parameters suitable for forward dynamics simulation, modelbased control, trajectory optimization, state estimation, and
other robotics algorithms that explicitly require a physically
valid mass matrix. Simulation results in forward dynamics
and model-based control demonstrate that the proposed
parameter sets are not only accurate for inverse dynamics
torque prediction but also enable reliable dynamic simulation
and control. The reported reference parameters therefore
provide a practical resource for the robotics community,
facilitating reproducible research and the development of
high-fidelity dynamic models of the UR5e and UR10e
platforms.
The identified inertial parameters show a high degree of
consistency across different robots of the same type, whereas
the friction parameters show noticeably larger variations.
This indicates that the inertial properties, determined by
the common mechanical design, are well represented while
friction is influenced by robot-specific drivetrain characteristics, which changes over time and with temperature.
Consequently, the averaged inertial parameter sets provide
robust nominal reference models for the UR5e and UR10e.
For the evaluated UR10e trajectory, the joint torques predicted using the averaged and robot-specific parameter sets
differ by less than approximately 5 % of the maximum joint
torques. Nevertheless, robot-specific friction identification
remains advantageous for applications requiring the highest
prediction accuracy, such as high-performance feed-forward
control.
It was further shown that for a typical pick-and-place
motion, the Coriolis and centrifugal torque contributions are
sufficiently small to be neglected without a significant loss of
accuracy. This observation is specific to motions with comparable velocity profiles. This simplification substantially
reduces the computational cost of evaluating the equations
VOLUME 14, 2026

of motion, decreasing the mean evaluation time from 8.9 µs
to 3.3 µs per sample.
The principal contribution of this work is the provision of
complete, physically consistent reference inertial parameter
sets for the UR5e and UR10e that are directly applicable
to rigid-body dynamics algorithms requiring body-specific
inertial properties. In contrast to base-parameters, the proposed models are suitable not only for inverse dynamics but
also for forward dynamics simulation, model-based control,
trajectory optimization, and URDF-based robot modeling.
Beyond the reported datasets, the presented identification
methodology provides a general framework for obtaining
physically consistent inertial parameters of serial manipulators whenever appropriate structural assumptions can be
formulated. The physically consistent identification approach
employed in this paper has also been successfully applied to a
parallel robot in [52], demonstrating its applicability beyond
serial manipulators.
